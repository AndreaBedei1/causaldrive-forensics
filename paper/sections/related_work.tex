\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

Reconstructing a collision from vehicle recordings connects accident
investigation with cooperative perception and multisensor estimation.
We review these strands through three recurring tasks: aligning recordings,
matching vehicle identities, and recovering motion.

\subsection{Accident Reconstruction from Recorded Evidence}

Simulation tools such as PC-Crash reconstruct vehicle motion and contacts
through dynamics models~\cite{steffan1996pccrash}; EDR measurements provide
independent checks on reconstruction estimates~\cite{gabler2004edr}.
DeepCrashTest extracts trajectories from monocular dashcam videos to create
virtual crash tests~\cite{bashetty2020deepcrashtest}, while Zhang et al.
combine vehicle logs and road geometry to reproduce pre-crash scenarios
in OpenSCENARIO~\cite{zhang2025reverse}. Trajectory recovery and replay
therefore have established forensic and testing applications.

Several works are particularly close to the present setting. Young et al.
reconstruct host and neighbouring trajectories from an EDR storing states
received through cooperative collision-warning
messages~\cite{young2009reconstruction}. Beck et al. process an automated
vehicle's sensor and motion recordings into investigator-facing
visualizations, including uninstrumented surrounding
vehicles~\cite{beck2023pipeline}. Their demonstrated tracking uses image
detections and depth, with a user-selected target; synchronization is
explicitly outside the study's scope. Matsumura et al. combine EDR and dashcam
data from one or both vehicles involved in a crash~\cite{matsumura2024precrash}.
Yuan et al. align and fuse EDR, GPS, and roadside video evidence for
simulation-based reconstruction~\cite{yuan2026multisource}.
These precedents establish the value of perception logs, multiple recordings,
and temporal alignment. The additional inference here concerns unknown
clock offsets and target correspondences across independently collected
radar recordings.

\subsection{Cooperative Perception and Tracking}

High-level cooperative perception already combines communicated object
tracks~\cite{rauch2012car2x}, estimates correspondences between vehicles'
object lists~\cite{rauch2013association}, and addresses spatial and temporal
alignment~\cite{allig2019alignment}. Aeberhard et al. explicitly fuse
asynchronous automotive tracks~\cite{aeberhard2012asynchronous}.
DAIR-V2X introduces time-compensated late fusion~\cite{yu2022dairv2x},
and V2X-ViT handles asynchronous sharing and pose errors in cooperative
detection~\cite{xu2022v2xvit}. Chiu et al. learn detection uncertainty within
a multisensor Kalman framework for cooperative tracking~\cite{chiu2024dmstrack}.
More recently, Castelino et al. compare track-fusion strategies using
Collective Perception Messages in asynchronous scenarios, assuming reliable
source timestamps~\cite{castelino2026fusion}.
Asynchronous fusion and cross-vehicle association are therefore established.
Here, separately collected recordings have unknown clock offsets and target
correspondences; inter-vehicle communication during the incident is not
required. The objective is a retrospective account of the collision and
each recorder's evidence history.

\subsection{Association, Fusion, and Temporal Registration}

Track association and fusion must account for correlated estimation
errors~\cite{barshalom1981correlation,barshalom1986process}.
Covariance intersection provides an established means of combining estimates
with unknown cross-correlations~\cite{julier1997intersection}.
Extended-object radar tracking models multiple reflection centres on one
vehicle~\cite{hammarstrand2012extended}, while consistent cooperative
localization addresses relative pose and correlation
uncertainty~\cite{hery2021localization}.
Our association tests build on these principles: one rigid body must explain
the surfaces seen by different observers, with uncertainty and competing
identities considered explicitly. Later evidence can establish earlier
identities while preserving the original local observations and event times.

Recovering alignment from common scene dynamics~\cite{caspi2002alignment}
and estimating sensor timing passively~\cite{olson2010synchronization}
also predate this work. The present method uses shared collision reports,
tested against recorded geometry and available radar motion, to estimate
constant clock offsets. When collision evidence cannot align a recorder,
the method compares radar observations of a recording vehicle with that
vehicle's own logged motion and footprint to estimate the time offset.
This couples temporal registration to the physical evidence used for
vehicle association.

\subsection{Forensic Timelines and Interpretation}

The NTSB's Tempe investigation demonstrates the forensic value of relating
an automated vehicle's perception history to its subsequent
response~\cite{ntsb2019tempe}. Response models also show that the timing of
an action depends on the evolving situation and driver
expectations~\cite{engstrom2024response}. Counterfactual studies, such as
Scanlon et al.'s reconstructed fatal crashes, assess alternative outcomes
through additional simulation~\cite{scanlon2021fatal}.
Our contribution concerns the evidence supporting such analyses: it combines
clock recovery, cross-observer identity association, reconstruction of vehicles
without recordings, and a shared event chronology retaining each recorder's
observation and response history. This chronology supports expert analysis;
it does not establish driver awareness, avoidability, or responsibility.
