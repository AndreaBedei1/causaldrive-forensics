\section{Running Example}
\label{sec:running-example}

Consider two cars travelling in the same direction in adjacent lanes
(Fig.~\ref{fig:cut-in-example}). B, slightly ahead and slower than A, moves
into A's lane. Moments later, A hits B from behind. \emph{Who caused the crash?}
The rear-end impact invites blame on A; the cut-in invites blame on B.
Neither fact tells us how much room B left when it crossed into A's lane.
%
\begin{figure}[!b]
  \centering
  \input{figures/cut_in_example}
  \caption{A cut-in followed by a collision. The outcome alone does not
  distinguish an insufficient gap from a delayed response; both may occur.}
  \label{fig:cut-in-example}
\end{figure}
%
If B merged with very little clearance, even a prompt response from A might
not have prevented the collision. If B left enough room, A may have had time
to slow down but reacted late, perhaps because the driver was distracted.
A tight merge and a delayed response could also have acted together.

The crucial evidence lies in the approach: when B began changing lanes,
where both cars were as B entered A's lane, how quickly the gap closed, and
when A began braking or steering. Either car's view alone may miss part of
this sequence. Even a complete account of the vehicles' movements cannot
reveal what A's driver actually noticed.
